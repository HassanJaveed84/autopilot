**No. For generally healthy adults over 50 who live at home, the best evidence says vitamin D supplements do not prevent fractures.** The evidence is fairly strong, because it comes from large randomized trials and from several pooled analyses of them.

**What the evidence shows**
- **The largest U.S. trial found no effect.** VITAL gave 2,000 IU of vitamin D3 daily to men 50 or older and women 55 or older [1]. Total fractures were no different from placebo (hazard ratio 0.98; 95% CI 0.89 to 1.08) [1]. There were 25,871 participants followed for a median of 5.3 years, and hip and nonvertebral fractures also did not differ. [2]
- **The result held in subgroups.** Even among people with low blood levels (under 20 ng/mL), there was no effect on fracture risk. [3] A news report on the trial adds that the 25% of participants who also took calcium did not benefit either [4].
- **The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) reviewed 20 trials in 2024.** The pooled risk ratio for hip fracture was 0.99 (95% CI 0.86 to 1.13), meaning no fewer hip fractures per 1,000 people supplemented. [5] Its draft recommendation was against vitamin D, with or without calcium, to prevent fractures in postmenopausal women and men 60 or older [6]. It rated this moderate certainty of no net benefit [6].
- **Earlier pooled analyses agree.** A JAMA review of 33 trials with 51,145 participants found no lower fracture risk with calcium, vitamin D, or both in community-dwelling older adults [7]. An umbrella review noted that reviews limited to community-dwelling people found no significant fracture reduction [8].

**Where the evidence is less clear**
- **Older trials and calcium combinations.** Some meta-analyses of calcium plus vitamin D did find fewer fractures. Eight of 12 reviews reported lower hip fracture risk when they combined institutionalized and community-dwelling people. [8] I infer that the benefit seen there comes mainly from frail, institutionalized, or deficient groups. Healthy people living at home don't show it.
- **A possible late benefit.** One analysis suggested a possible benefit after about 3.5 years [3], but a review noted it was unclear whether this was real, given its inconsistency with other studies [3].
- **Small harms.** The USPSTF evidence review found a very small increase in kidney stones with vitamin D, with or without calcium [9].
- **The 2024 USPSTF statement was a draft.** The sources I found describe it as a draft from December 2024. I didn't find a final version, so confirm that on the USPSTF site. Its 2018 final recommendation had already advised against lower doses and found insufficient evidence for higher doses [10].

**Who this does not apply to**
These findings cover healthy people. The USPSTF says they don't apply to people with a history of osteoporotic fractures, conditions linked to vitamin D deficiency or malabsorption, or a diagnosis of osteoporosis or vitamin D deficiency [6]. They also exclude people in nursing homes or other institutions. If any of those fit you, or you take vitamin D on a doctor's advice, talk to your clinician before changing anything.

In my judgment, routine vitamin D pills are not a good way for an otherwise healthy person to try to prevent fractures. Other approaches have a stronger case. The USPSTF recommends exercise to prevent falls in older adults at increased risk [11], and screening for osteoporosis in women 65 or older and in younger women at increased risk [11].
